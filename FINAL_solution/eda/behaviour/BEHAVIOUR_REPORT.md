# Behavioural & business EDA: verification and new findings

**Scope.** This report covers how users behave over time, how they use credit, and which segments and patterns matter for the business. It sits alongside `FINAL_solution/REPORT.md` and checks its Bonus findings A1–A4, B1–B4, C1, D1–D2, E1 and E3–E5. It does not cover column-level forensics.

**Data.** Clean extract: 204,590 rows and 18,000 users, with TXN00204591–TXN00204694 dropped and "Other services" merged into "Other Services".

**Conventions.** These are unchanged from the existing solution:
- Retention is measured in calendar months.
- Churn means active in month *t* and no transaction in *t+1*.
- Light / medium / heavy = 1 / 2–3 / 4+ transactions in the month.

**Method.**
- Every number comes from `behaviour_deep_dive.py`, which writes `outputs/eda_numbers.json` with 326 keys.
- All logits on user-month panels use user-clustered standard errors.
- A Holm correction is applied to the headline tests (`outputs/multiple_testing_holm.csv`). Every effect called significant below survives it.
- All findings are observational.

**One structural fact explains most of what follows.** The extract behaves as if it were generated by a few simple, independent rules:
1. Each user has a **stable personal purchase rate**, and churn risk is tied to that rate.
2. Purchases fall **uniformly at random within each active calendar month**.
3. Ticket size is an **independent draw that is the same for everyone**.
4. Each purchase's category is an **independent draw from the user's own fixed mix**.
5. The balance shows a **35% per-purchase "repayment" coin flip**.

Behaviour that "looks" meaningful beyond these rules (waves, rhythms, journeys, credit hunger, repayment styles) turns out to be arithmetic. That is the main message for the Bonus section: it should stress what is *real and actionable* (the frequency trait and the inflow) and drop or reframe patterns that are artefacts.

## Part 2: New findings (ranked by business value)

### N1. Churn risk is a stable personal trait (how often a user buys), not a monthly state

**Headline.** A user's 3-month average frequency predicts churn. Once that average is known, this month's dip or spike adds nothing, and churners' last month looks like their other months.

**Numbers.** Sample: user-months with three full prior months, n=21,798.
- **Lag test.** In `churn ~ log n_t + log n_t−1 + log n_t−2 + tenure`, the coefficients are −0.088 (t), −0.116 (t−1) and −0.137 (t−2). The current month is *not* more predictive than older months (Wald t = t−1: p=0.48; t = t−2: p=0.23). If churn reacted to this month's activity, n_t would dominate.
- **Deviation test.** In `churn ~ log(3-month avg) + log(n_t / 3-month avg)`, the average has coefficient −0.35 (p<10⁻²⁹). The deviation has coefficient +0.04 (95% CI −0.03 to +0.11, p=0.28). The CI rules out more than about a **0.4pp** churn reduction from a month with twice the user's usual purchases.
- **Same average, different months.** Within users whose 3-month average is 2–3.3, churn is 22.3% / 22.6% / 21.7% after a 1 / 2–3 / 4+ transaction month.
- **Within-user check.** Churners' final month has +0.03 transactions versus their own other full months (±0.05; Wilcoxon p=0.25; n=5,049). The placebo (still-active users' Jun-25) gives +0.05 (p=0.97).
- **Spread of risk.** The 3-month average separates churn more than a single month: 32.5% (avg ≤1) vs 19.0% (avg 6+), against 27.1% vs 20.6% for this month's count. Both are weak classifiers (AUC 0.553 vs 0.535).

**Robustness.**
- User-clustered standard errors.
- The result holds with linear counts as well as logs.
- The measurement-error concern runs the other way: a noisy average would make n_t look *more* predictive, not less.

**So what.**
1. **Segment and target on a 3-month average** (reliability 0.71 vs 0.45 for one month). The Jun-25 risk tiers in 2.3 use one month.
2. **Frequency rewards.** The data give no sign that a one-month bump in purchases lowers churn. The "+390 MAU" ceiling in 2.5 assumes users' *lasting* habits change. The test should therefore read churn **2–3 months after the incentive ends**, not purchases in the incentive month. It should also be judged on durable change in frequency.
3. This also explains why the AUC is 0.56: risk is a slowly revealed trait, not an event.

**Confidence.** High.

### N2. The "onboarding waves" and the "partial first month" are arithmetic, not behaviour

**Headline.** New users' first-purchase days are exactly what you get by scattering their month-0 purchases uniformly over the whole month and taking the earliest one. Existing users' first purchase of any month clusters the same way. The extract contains no evidence of month-start onboarding batches.

**Numbers.**
- **Early-month clustering matches the expectation.** First purchase on days 1–3: 25.4% observed vs 25.2% expected from "minimum of n0 uniform days". By day 15: 76.3% vs 75.8%.
- **Existing users look the same.** Continuing users' first purchase of the month falls on days 1–3 in 27.0% of months (27.3% expected).
- **Same distribution for M0 and later months.** Given the month's purchase count, the first-purchase day in M0 has the same distribution as in later months (KS p ≥ 0.14 for n=1…6). For example, n=3 gives a mean of 8.06 vs 8.08.
- **M0 is not a partial month.** Users active in M0 and M1 make **3.12 vs 3.12** transactions (paired p=0.89). If late joiners only had part of M0, n0 would be clearly below n1 on average.
- **Join day carries no information beyond frequency.** Raw, a late join (after day 20) raises later churn (+0.24 log-odds, p<10⁻¹⁰). Controlling for n0 and running frequency, the join-day terms vanish (joint p=0.90). For M1 retention given n0, the joint p is 0.12.

**Robustness.**
- Expectations are computed per month length.
- KS tests are run by count.
- The test was replicated on continuing months.

**So what.**
- Reframe Option B's "strongest growth pattern" as **restoring inflow**. Drop the *wave* story ("eligibility batches or campaign waves at month start") unless the funnel data show real start-of-month approvals.
- Ask Growth for true **approval / activation dates**: in this extract, "join day" is just the user's first purchase.
- In Test A, keep stratifying on join day, but describe it as stratifying on a frequency proxy.
- In Q1c, describe the day-1–3 subgroup as heavy-user-selected.
- *Caveat:* in production, real sign-up dates would make M0 partial. The point is that this extract cannot show waves.

**Confidence.** High that the pattern is arithmetic in this extract. Unknown for production.

### N3. Tenure buys nothing: users neither grow nor settle; their trajectory is set from the start

**Headline.** Within a user, frequency, ticket and category mix are flat across tenure. Within a frequency band, churn is flat across tenure.

**Numbers.**
- **Frequency.** The within-user slope (user fixed effects, 6,612 users) is −0.0002 transactions per tenure month (95% CI −0.009 to +0.008), so at most ±0.1 transactions over a year.
- **Ticket.** Ticket vs tenure: Spearman 0.002 (p=0.29).
- **Category mix.** Category mix vs tenure shows a ≤1.2pp maximum deviation. A naive chi-square gives p<0.001, but it ignores clustering by user: a within-user permutation test gives **p=0.16**.
- **Churn.** Churn vs tenure given running frequency: −0.006 per month (p=0.20). In the 2.5–3.5 band, churn is 22.2% at M2 and 21.0% at M9+. The average frequency of active users still rises from 3.12 (M1) to 3.79 (M9+), purely because light users leave.

**So what.**
- Loyalty programmes that reward tenure have no natural audience here. Tenure is not a risk factor once frequency is known.
- Because the hazard is flat within a band, a simple **geometric value model by frequency band** is defensible for budgeting. It should be calibrated on realised data (see N5 for why).
- Value tiers can be assigned once 2–3 months of frequency are seen.

**Confidence.** High.

### N4. User value = how often × how long; ticket size is not a user trait (there is no "big spender" segment)

**Headline.** Average ticket varies across users no more than chance. Lifetime GMV differences come from tenure and frequency.

**Numbers.**
- **Ticket is not a trait.** Ticket-size ICC is −0.0003 (one-way ANOVA F=0.99, p=0.65, users with ≥5 txns). The split-half correlation of a user's mean log ticket is **−0.007**.
- **What drives lifetime GMV.** Variance of log lifetime GMV (complete lifetimes): **50% active months, 35% frequency, 16% average ticket**. The ticket share is sampling noise, because ICC≈0.
- **Top 10% of complete lifetimes.** They have 8.0 vs 2.7 active months and 5.1 vs 2.7 transactions per month. Their ticket is 179K vs 168K (+7%, a lucky-draw selection effect), and their limit 5.24M vs 5.22M.
- **Monthly GMV tracks count.** Corr(monthly GMV, monthly count) is 0.86.

**So what.**
- Don't target or price on past ticket size or limit.
- A ticket-upsell campaign has no natural audience, because no user is reliably a big-ticket user.
- An RFM model here collapses to F and R. "M" is roughly F × 170K VND, and k-means on these features would just slice frequency.
- Retention budget per user should scale with the user's frequency band (N5).

**Confidence.** High.

### N5. Put heavy users first in the silence trigger: reach them about 5 days earlier at the same precision; they carry over a quarter of the GMV lost to churn

**Headline.** For users averaging >5 purchases a month, silence by **day 10** is as diagnostic as silence by day 15 is for everyone (59% vs 61% lost). These users are 11% of churners but 27% of churned GMV.

**Numbers.** Bands use the 3-month average.

| Band | P(no purchase rest of month \| silent by day 10) | by day 15 | Share of churners | Share of churned GMV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ≤1.5 | 48% | 58% | 25.0% | 9.4% |
| 1.5–3 | 46% | 58% | 41.7% | 32.9% |
| 3–5 | 47% | 62% | 21.9% | 30.4% |
| **>5** | **59%** (95% CI 57–61%) | **78%** | **11.4%** | **27.3%** |
| All users | 48% | 61% | | |

- **Model-free value check.** Realised Jan–Jun-25 GMV per Dec-24 active user is 0.78M (≤1.5), 1.18M (1.5–3), 1.88M (3–5) and **2.94M (>5)**, i.e. 3.8× from bottom to top. The >5 and 3–5 bands are 41% of users and **59% of the next six months' GMV**.
- **Why model-free.** A geometric model by band over-states this spread out of time: light users are under-predicted ×0.48 and heavy users over-predicted ×1.18, because a 3-month band regresses to the mean. Use realised figures, not the model.
- **Size of the segment.** There are 479 users with average >5 in Jun-25.

**So what.**
- Make Test B's trigger day **depend on the band**: day ~10 for >5, ~13 for 3–5, day 15 for the rest.
- Measure the readout in **GMV**, not only in users.
- The >5 arm is small (~170 silent per month), so it serves as a pre-registered subgroup, not its own powered test.

**Robustness.** Precision CIs are Wilson intervals. The values are realised out of time. The mechanism is simple (heavy stayers buy early in the month, so their silence is more informative), which makes it likely to transfer. Whether a reminder *changes* anything still needs the test.

**Confidence.** High for the targeting arithmetic; the uplift is unknown.

### N6. `total_outstanding` contains no repayment behaviour, and the limit isn't enforced: no credit-behaviour segmentation is possible from this file

**Headline.** The balance "repayment" is a coin flip on each purchase, the same for every user and at every moment. Revolvers vs transactors, repayment cadence and never-repayers cannot be identified.

**Numbers.** These exclude the 4,043 rows that touch the limit cap.
- **The coin flip.** P(balance falls at a purchase) = **35.0%**.
- **The same for every user.** Over-dispersion across 6,137 users is **1.00** (p=0.48).
- **No memory.** P(repay | repaid last time) = 35.0% vs P(repay | not) = 35.0%.
- **No link to anything observed.** Repayment is unrelated to:
  - days since the last purchase;
  - day of month and weekday;
  - tenure and calendar month;
  - limit, prior utilisation and amount.
  All chi-square p ≥ 0.18.
- **The amount repaid.** It is a uniform 30–100% share of (balance + purchase): KS p=0.97.
- **Full payoff is rare.** There are only 111 balance-to-zero events.
- **Never-repayers are chance.** 2,899 users never show a repayment, against **2,811 expected by chance**.
- **The "balance left by churners" is mechanical.** The balance after the last purchase rises with lifetime purchases, from 128K (1 purchase) to 564K (20+), and is never observed to be paid, because repayment is only visible at a later purchase. 6.12B VND in total; median utilisation at exit 6.5%.
- **The limit is not enforced.** 4,032 purchases exceeded headroom, and amounts are the same near the cap.

**So what.**
- Don't build repayment, revolver or "credit-hungry" features on this field. That means dropping E3 and reframing E4.
- Request **statement, payment and DPD tables** from Risk. They are the prerequisite for any credit-behaviour insight, for a limit-increase test, and for the Cash Loan cross-sell question.
- Flag "purchases approved above the limit" to the data owner: either the cap is applied only to the stored balance, or authorisation ignores the limit.

**Confidence.** High that the field is uninformative. What it means in production is unknown.

### N7. Category choice is memoryless: no journeys, no sticky merchants, no next-best category

**Headline.** The next purchase's category is predicted by the user's own mix and by nothing else, not even by the previous purchase.

**Numbers.** Users with ≥6 txns.
- **Same package as the previous purchase.** Observed **52.5%**, against **52.6%** expected from the user's own mix.
- **Same carrier.** 42.1% vs 42.2%.
- **Same-day pairs.** 42.0% vs 42.0% (n=12,023).
- **Home category vs frequency and churn.** Home share is similar across home packages (62–67%, users with ≥10 txns). Home package doesn't predict frequency (ANOVA p=0.25) or churn (p=0.56). Home carrier doesn't predict churn either (21 carriers, p=0.50; raw range 21.5–25.8%).

**So what.**
- "Next-best-category" or journey models will find nothing in this data.
- Cross-sell should be judged only on incremental GMV in a test.
- For CRM, "offer in the user's home category" is the right default: it is the only category signal there is.

**Confidence.** High.

### N8. Early value signals: first-purchase attributes say nothing; the first full month's frequency separates groups but not individuals

**Headline.** First-purchase amount, category and limit don't predict future value. M1 frequency moves the group average 2.6×, but explains only about 2–3% of individual variance.

**Numbers.** Users from the Jan–Jul-24 cohorts who were active in M1 (n=6,386); outcome is months 2–11.
- **First-purchase attributes.** Amount, package, limit and join band give R² **0.4%** for log GMV and 0.7% for active months. Individually: amount p=0.56, limit p=0.10, package p=0.49.
- **M0 and M1 counts.** R² 2.1% (GMV) and 3.1% (months).
- **Group averages.** M1 with 1 transaction → 0.99M VND and 2.4 active months in M2–M11; M1 with 4+ → **2.61M VND and 3.5 months**.

**So what.**
- Use cohort- and band-level averages for CAC/LTV.
- Individual LTV scoring from transactions alone won't work; whether a user stays is mostly not in this data.
- Don't vary onboarding offers by the first purchase's category or amount.

**Confidence.** High for the nulls; medium for the size of the M1 group effect, because it overlaps the existing Q1c value context.

### N9. Cohorts behave identically, so the decline is not a behaviour shift

**Headline.** 2025 cohorts look exactly like 2024 cohorts on every behaviour measured.

**Numbers.**

| | 2024H1 | 2024H2 | 2025H1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean n0 | 3.03 | 3.01 | 3.04 |
| Share with n0 = 1 | 30.8% | 31.6% | 30.9% |
| M1 retention | 72.9% | 73.0% | 73.1% |
| Frequency in M1–M3 | 3.20 | 3.17 | 3.23 |
| Churn in M1–M3 | 24.2% | 25.2% | 25.0% |
| Repayment-flip rate (H1s only) | 35.0% | | 35.2% |

- The frequency→churn gradient does not differ by cohort (interaction LR p=0.26).

**So what.** This supports the main report: the decline comes from **how many** users arrive, not from **who** they are.

**Confidence.** High.

## Part 4: Script

- **Script:** `FINAL_solution/eda/behaviour/behaviour_deep_dive.py`
- **Run:** `python3 -I behaviour_deep_dive.py`. It takes about 1 minute; the 200-draw within-user permutation test is the slow part.
- **Requirements:** pandas, numpy, scipy, statsmodels, scikit-learn.
- **Input:** read-only, from `data.csv` in the project root.
- **Outputs** (all in `outputs/`):
  - `eda_numbers.json`: every number quoted here, with key prefixes `a*/b*/c*/d*/e*` for verification and `n1…n9` for new findings;
  - CSV tables (`v_*` for verification, `n*` for new findings, and `multiple_testing_holm.csv`);
  - `run_log.txt`.
- **Exploration helper:** `load.py` is a small loader used during exploration. The main script is self-contained.
